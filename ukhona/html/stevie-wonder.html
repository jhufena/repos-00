Part I · The Verse · D♭ Major · Line Cliché & Initial Entropy
D♭ Major · I

The Verse: Chromatic Inner-Voice Descent

Progression: D♭maj7 · B♭m7 · E♭m7 · A♭11 → then the cliché

The verse opens on a I – vi – ii – V turnaround so clean it sounds almost domestic — which is exactly the point. The lyrics place us in front of a mirror, noticing. The harmony mirrors that mundanity.

But then, on the ii chord (E♭m), Stevie deploys the minor line cliché. The bass holds on E♭ while the inner voice descends chromatically: E♭ → D → D♭. Harmonically this creates:

Chord Function Emotional Register
E♭m ii in D♭ Stable — the mundane daily life
E♭m(maj7) ii with raised 7th Agonizing dissonance — D♮ against E♭ minor triad
E♭m7 Release of tension Brief exhale before dominant
A♭7 → D♭maj7 V → I resolution Home, but we've been marked by the descent
"The movement from E♭m to E♭m(maj7) is the first sign of the loss function increasing — the internal dissonance of a man realizing his model is poorly calibrated for the coming reality."

The major 7th raised inside a minor chord creates what theorists call a dissonant passing tone — a note that belongs nowhere it touches, yet makes perfect emotional sense. It is "very slowly picking me apart" rendered as harmony.

Part II · The Chorus · Modal Interchange · The Heartbreak Chord
The minor iv chord (G♭m6) is borrowed from D♭ minor — a technique called modal interchange. In jazz and R&B, this chord carries a specific emotional weight: the sound of hope curdling.
Chorus · IV → iv

The Chorus: Hope Followed Immediately by Its Collapse

G♭maj7 · G♭m6 · Fm7 · B♭7sus4 – B♭7 (IV · iv · iii · VI7)

The chorus lifts off on G♭maj7 (IV) — a feeling of openness, of "well, I'm a man of many wishes." And then, with surgical cruelty, Stevie lowers the major third a half step. G♭maj7 becomes G♭m6.

This is modal interchange: borrowing the minor iv from the parallel minor key of D♭. It is the textbook heartbreak chord in jazz and R&B. It forces the melody to sigh downward precisely on "hope my premonition misses."

He doesn't wallow. He walks it diatonic to Fm7 (iii), then turns it into a secondary dominant: B♭7sus4 → B♭7, functioning as V/ii. The pull toward the ii chord is magnetic — this is classical voice leading, dressed in Motown clothes.

One chromatic alteration — from G♭ major to G♭ minor — is all it takes. The song is never again as safe as it was before that chord.

The Chorus Walk-Up: Borrowed Minor iv Mid-Climb

E♭m7 · Fm7 · G♭m7 · A♭11 · D♭maj7 (ii · iii · iv · V · I)

Rather than a plain ii – V – I cadence, Stevie walks the bass straight up the scale. The ear expects the IV chord to be major (G♭maj7 diatonically). Instead, it arrives as G♭m7 — minor, borrowed again from the parallel minor. The modal interchange infiltrates even the walk-up. Even the moment of return home is tinged with grief.

Part III · The Coda · Sequential Planing · Descent Disguised as Ascent
D♭ → A → B · Coda

The Modulation: Down a Whole Step, Into Resignation

The full climb: E♭m7 – Fm7 – G♭m7 – A♭m7 – Amaj7 – Bm7 – D♭m7 – G♭7(♯9) → Bmaj7

Instead of resolving the second chorus walk-up to D♭, Stevie keeps climbing. Then he breaks the key entirely, planing minor chords upward through chromatic space with no tonal anchor. It feels like forward motion. It is not.

Chord Role in Climb Tonal Interpretation
E♭m7 – Fm7 – G♭m7 Standard walk-up beginning ii – iii – iv in D♭ (familiar ground)
A♭m7 First break from D♭ Diatonic v minor — begins the drift
Amaj7 Pivot chord · bVI of D♭ Dark attractor — signals new gravitational center
Bm7 – D♭m7 ii – iii in A Major Building cadential approach to B
G♭7(♯9) → Bmaj7 V → I in B Major Hammer falls: new tonic a whole step below D♭
Pop music almost always modulates up — a key change as emotional lift, a final chorus that soars. Stevie modulates down. The body doesn't rise to meet the final refrain. It sinks. "This time could mean goodbye" is not a triumphant belt. It is a realization of defeat.

The path D♭ → A → B traces an unbroken stochastic descent. Each step feels like forward noise. The global trend leads to an emotional local minimum — a key the ear never saw coming, arriving not with fanfare but with the quiet thud of a door closing.

This is what sets Stevie apart. An AI can map the coordinates. The choice to modulate down — creating sinking rather than soaring — remains a uniquely human decision about what heartbreak actually feels like in the body.

The Algorithm Can Map It. Only the Human Chose It.

The chromatic inner-voice movement of the verse cliché, the borrowed minor iv of the chorus, the non-diatonic planing of the coda — each technique is nameable, teachable, reproducible. What cannot be reproduced is the instinct that knew a downward modulation would feel like defeat rather than resolution.

Music theory names what happened after the fact. Stevie chose it before a theory existed for it. The stochastic walk from D♭ to B Major is disorientingly coherent because it was felt before it was calculated. Each step was noise. The endpoint was inevitable.

A 45-year-old human ear still possesses a heuristic for soul that a billion tokens can only approximate. The map proves the territory was always there. Ukubona. To see, to witness — to look into the mirror. 🎹
